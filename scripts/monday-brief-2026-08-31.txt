Watch, read and listen. Three picks, and this week all three of them are real.

Start with the listen. Ninety-nine Percent Invisible, the episode called Spolia, published on the twenty-fifth of August. Twenty-eight minutes, which is one minute longer than your commute leg. You will finish it in the parking lot.

Register first. Calm, curious, faintly wry, and built as a journey rather than an argument.

It opens in Granby, a neighbourhood in South Liverpool that the local council spent decades emptying out. Before the authorities boarded up each house they stripped it, taking the copper, the lead, the plumbing, anything with a resale value. Then they nailed a sign to the front door reading, all items of value have been removed from this property.

A handful of residents refused to leave. They cleared the lots, planted gardens, painted the boarded houses in bright colours, and formed a community land trust to buy the empty houses back. A design collective joined them and started experimenting with the rubble. They ground the broken brick and slate from those same houses into a rough terrazzo, poured it into moulds, polished it smooth, and put it back into the rooms it came out of, as mantelpieces and countertops.

Then the episode pulls back and tells you that reusing the bones of old buildings in new ones has a name. Spolia. The Romans industrialised it. When the empire's quarries got hard to reach in the third century there was a great deal of expensive marble in Rome slowly falling apart from neglect, so Constantine took the carved panels off monuments two centuries older than he was, had the old emperors' faces recut to look like his own, and built himself a triumphal arch out of other men's victories.

It ends back in Liverpool, quietly, on the idea that the materials of the past are never worthless. New source for us, and the shortest thing that show has published in a month.

Now the read. Nonfiction this week, alternating off last week's novel.

An American Caddie in St. Andrews, by Oliver Horovitz.

Shape first, because that is what carries a nonfiction book. A boy's phone rings in the middle of his own high school graduation. It is Harvard. He is in, but he cannot start for a year. So he spends the gap year in St. Andrews, enrolls in the caddie trainee programme on the Old Course, and moves in with his great uncle Ken, who is ninety.

The journey is one season of looping, trainee badge to a real bag on the most famous golf course in the world. The people are the caddie shack, a closed shop of local men with no particular reason to be kind to an American teenager, who gradually are. The voice is young, funny about its own incompetence, and never sentimental about the place.

This is the golf lane, which is one of your two subject overrides, and it is the travel and ground end of it rather than the tour end. Nobody's swing gets analysed.

The comparison, said out loud. It sits closest to A Course Called Ireland. Same walking-around-Scotland-and-talking-to-people register, smaller and more personal. If the Coyne books worked on you because of the company, this works the same way.

Audiobook is nine hours and sixteen minutes, read by the author, which for a memoir this personal is the right call. That eats the same commute minutes as the listen pick, so print keeps both alive. The hook is immediate. The phone call is page one.

Caveat for the record. Nothing on file says whether you have read this, and Horovitz is on no list. Same low-risk judgement as Thorogood last week.

Golf. Lead with the ground.

The BMW Championship was at Bellerive, outside St. Louis, and Bellerive is a family argument in the shape of a golf course. Robert Trent Jones senior built it, it opened in nineteen sixty, and it was so long and so punishing they called it the Green Monster of Ladue Road. Five years later it became the youngest course ever to host a United States Open. Then in two thousand five his son, Rees Jones, rebuilt it. Lengthened it again, deepened the bunkers, shrank the greens slightly, and exaggerated the contours his father had put in them. A son editing his father's sentences and mostly agreeing with them.

Disney. One story, and it is a clean illustration of why the caveat matters.

At the D Twenty Three showcase this month, Thomas Mazloum, who runs Disney Experiences, told the room the company is bringing the Yeti back to life inside Expedition Everest. That part is confirmed, on the record, and it is genuinely news. The figure has been frozen under a strobe light since two thousand eight, which is why the fan press has spent the better part of twenty years calling it the Disco Yeti.

Here is the part that is not confirmed. There is no closing date. No reopening date. No word on how long Everest has to shut to do the work. In the days since, the fan sites have converged on two thousand twenty-eight as the earliest plausible window, reasoning that Disney will not close Everest until Tropical Americas is open and taking crowds. That reasoning is sensible. It is also entirely theirs. Disney has said none of it.

So the repair is real. The date is not. Do not book a trip around it.
