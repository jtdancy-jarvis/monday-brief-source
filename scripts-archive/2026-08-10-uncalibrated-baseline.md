Good morning, and welcome to the Monday Brief. Here's what actually earned a spot on this week's list — no filler, just the stuff worth carving out real time for. Nine picks this week, across podcasts, video, music, film, television, and books, and we'll move through them roughly in that order.

Let's start with audio. If you only have room for one podcast episode this week, make it Bad Blood: The Final Chapter. John Carreyrou — the reporter who broke the Theranos story in the first place — sits down to close out the Elizabeth Holmes saga, and it plays less like a victory lap than a genuinely uncomfortable reckoning with how far a confident voice and a good story can carry a fraud. If you lived through the original headlines back when they broke, this fills in the parts that never made it into the trial coverage, the slower and stranger details that got cut for time the first time around.

For something completely different, there's 100 Questions with Tom Simons. The premise sounds like a gimmick on paper — a single guest answers a hundred questions inside one hour — but in practice it forces people out of their rehearsed talking points fast. There's nowhere to hide behind a well-worn anecdote when the next question is already coming. The strongest episodes land when the guest is someone who doesn't usually do long-form interviews at all: an astronomer, a former gang leader, a late-night radio DJ. You tend to get somewhere real by question forty, mostly because nobody has the stamina to stay guarded that long.

On the documentary side, HBO's Monster of God is this week's pick. Eric Goode — the filmmaker behind Tiger King — turns his attention to the global exotic animal smuggling trade, and it has the same unsettling pull as that earlier work, minus the tabloid framing that made Tiger King easy to laugh off. This one's less interested in finding colorful characters to gawk at and more interested in how casually the trade actually operates in plain sight, how much of it hides behind ordinary-looking permits and paperwork rather than anything you'd recognize as criminal. Worth watching if you want something that'll quietly change how you look at a pet store window the next time you walk past one.

Now, music. Two records worth your Friday-morning listen this week, and they couldn't sit further apart from each other. Ceremony's seventh album, Tell Me Your Dream, is out now, produced by John Reis, and it leans harder into hardcore and post-punk territory than anything the band has put out in years. It's a tighter, meaner record than their last few efforts, and it's over before it has any chance to overstay its welcome — which, for a band this far into a career, is its own kind of compliment.

Citizen's Halcyon Blues is the other pick. They've always sat somewhere between emo and post-hardcore, and this record polishes up the hooks without sanding off the rawness that made their early material work in the first place. If you liked them a decade ago and quietly drifted away since, this is the record built to pull you back in.

On film, The Last Sunrise is out now on Prime Video, adapted from the bestselling novel about a college student with a chronic illness who falls for someone during a summer trip to Mallorca. It's the kind of premise that could tip into melodrama in the wrong hands, but the early word is that it plays things straighter and sadder than the marketing around it suggests, and that's usually the better outcome for a story like this one.

And for television, Ricky Gervais has a new one out: Alley Cats, an adult animated comedy on Netflix about a group of scheming British cats, in case that description alone hasn't already told you whether it's for you. It's exactly as dry and as profane as you'd expect from him at this point, and if his particular sense of humor still works on you, it's a quick, easy watch to burn an evening on.

Also worth noting, on the other end of the tone spectrum: Power Book III: Raising Kanan wraps up its run on Starz this week with its series finale. It's easy to forget how much this show quietly earned its ending, spinning out of the Power universe into something with its own voice, its own pacing, its own sense of when to let a scene breathe instead of rushing to the next plot beat. If you've been following Kanan's story since the beginning, this is the one to clear an evening for. And if you haven't, it's a reasonable argument for going back and starting from season one before the finale gets spoiled for you by everyone else's takes.

Last category, books. If you're already deep into the Inheritance Games world, The Brothers Hawthorne — the fourth book in Jennifer Lynn Barnes's series — is out now, and it does what a good spinoff is supposed to do: it hands the puzzle-box mystery over to a new set of narrators without losing what made the original trilogy so addictive to begin with.

And if crime fiction is more your speed, Val McDermid has a new one out too, called This Door of the Seas. She's one of the few writers working in the genre long enough at this point to actually subvert her own formula instead of just repeating it book after book, and early reactions suggest this is one of the stronger entries she's put out in a while.

That's the list for this week. A documentary that'll unsettle you a little more than you expect, two hardcore-adjacent records that sound nothing alike, a series finale years in the making, a book series worth finally catching up on, and a podcast that closes out one of the strangest business stories of the last decade. There's more here than any one person can actually get through in a week, and that's fine — that's kind of the point of a list like this. Pick one, maybe two, the ones that actually snag your attention when you hear them out loud, and let the rest go. Nobody's grading you on completion.

Have a good week, and I'll see you back here next Monday.
